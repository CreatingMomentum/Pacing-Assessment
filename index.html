Pacing self-assessment

This tool helps you identify where your energy is draining most, across physical, cognitive, emotional, sensory, and autonomic domains. There are no right or wrong answers. Choose the response that reflects your typical day, so not your best or your worst. For your privacy, answers provided aren't saved or stored.

❤
Physical pacing
Movement, tasks, and your body's workload
I break physical tasks into smaller steps with planned rest in between.
I stop a task before symptoms arrive, rather than continuing until I feel worse.
I sit or lie down during activities when possible, for example doing tasks at a table rather than standing.
I factor in hidden load, like poor sleep, a higher resting heart rate, pain, or temperature changes, when planning my day.
I use mobility aids or supportive tools (compression, chairs, trolleys) to conserve energy rather than pushing through without them.
🧠
Cognitive pacing
Mental effort, focus, and brain fog
I schedule cognitively demanding tasks, like admin, emails, reading, or decisions, during the time of day when my brain is clearest.
I treat admin, planning, and emails as real activities that cost energy, not as easy or low-effort tasks.
I use short, focused work blocks with deliberate rest in between, rather than working for as long as I can.
I limit screen time and multitasking, for example keeping only one thing open at a time and avoiding task-switching.
I stop at the first signs of brain fog or irritability, rather than pushing through hoping it will pass.
💕
Emotional pacing
Emotional energy and nervous system load
I reduce my exposure to stressful conversations or emotionally heavy situations when I'm already depleted.
I'm mindful of the energy cost of attention-grabbing content, like social media, news, or emotionally intense shows, and set limits around it.
I give space to emotions when they show up, rather than suppressing them to keep going.
I use a grounding or calming practice when I start to feel overwhelmed, before it escalates.
After an emotionally intense event, even a positive one, I plan in recovery time and reduce other demands on myself.
👁
Sensory pacing
Light, sound, and environmental stimulation
I actively reduce bright lights, loud sounds, or visual clutter in my environment during the day.
I notice sensory overload in the moment, rather than only realising after I've crashed.
I use tools like sunglasses, earplugs, or noise-cancelling headphones to reduce sensory load.
I limit my time in busy, unpredictable, or high-stimulation environments and plan around them where I can.
I count background stimulation, like a TV on in the background, open windows onto a busy street, or bright overhead lighting, as part of my daily sensory budget.
☯
Autonomic pacing
Nervous system regulation and body signals
I recognise early warning signs that my autonomic nervous system is struggling, like increased heart rate, dizziness, feeling flushed, or feeling 'wired but tired,' and I rest before symptoms escalate.
I sit down and elevate my legs when I can, rather than staying upright for long periods.
I stay hydrated and have discussed electrolyte use with my doctor or healthcare provider.
I eat smaller, more regular meals with protein rather than large carb-rich meals, knowing that digestion affects my energy levels.
I factor in temperature changes, like heat, cold, or humidity, when planning my day.
